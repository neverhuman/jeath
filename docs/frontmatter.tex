\textbf{Abstract.}\enspace
AI control protocols act on proposals: a monitor reads what an agent proposes, and an agent that is caught is stopped.
Stopping the model does not stop the effects it has already dispatched. We hold authority as state, checked where
effects commit, and compare three stops: halting inference, revoking admission at a broker, and fencing the sink so
that it refuses work issued before the stop. On real broker and sink processes driven by a synthetic agent,
\vsixHaltRange{} effects committed after a halt took effect and \vsixRevokeRange{} after a revocation; after a fence
\vsixFenceRange{} did, as designed, and \vsixFenceLost{} legitimate calls were discarded. Counted from the decision, the
leak is about \(\lambda(L+w)\) while executors keep up (call rate \(\lambda\), enforcement delay \(L\), tool latency
\(w\)): a fence removes \(\lambda w\), and only faster enforcement removes \(\lambda L\). Review misses violations that
live in state. Given the rules and the state, a \vsixEFiveCkpt{} reviewer replying with a bare verdict accepted a
proposal from the approved list that the state had since made unauthorized, in all \vsixExactCases{} cases; reviews
that reasoned for a median of \vsixReasonerTokens{} tokens accepted \vsixReasonerExact. A written specification pays in legitimate
work instead. On AgentDojo, one written without the benchmark's solutions admitted the reference solution of
\vsixStageStrictUser{} tasks; with \vsixEEightCkpt{} it completed \vsixStrictUtility{} benign tasks, against
\vsixUnprotectedUtility{} unprotected and \vsixProgentUtility{} under Progent. It was written knowing the public
injections, which succeeded in \vsixStrictNative{} episodes (\vsixUnprotectedNative{} unprotected); injections written
to stay inside it succeeded in \vsixStrictInside. Of \vsixFenceMechanisms{} fencing mechanisms probed on PostgreSQL,
etcd and a filesystem, only \vsixFenceAllThree{} passed all three probes. All models are small open-weight checkpoints
run locally.
