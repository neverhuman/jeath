\section{Evidence Lifetime Across Model Upgrades}\label{sec:gaps}

Measurements on 4B and 8B open-weight checkpoints need not transfer to stronger models. That objection is decisive for claims about a model's behaviour, and it sorts this paper's evidence: \emph{evidence has an upgrade lifetime}. A quantity conditioned on a model's distribution of proposals expires when the weights, prompts or scaffolding change. A property proved over every input the sink can receive, and enforced outside the model's process, does not depend on the proposer at all, but it is only as broad as the specification it proves and holds only where the assumptions under it hold.

Table~\ref{tab:upgrade} sorts the paper's claims by upgrade lifetime. One row deserves emphasis. A complete gate's unauthorized rate is the same at every checkpoint because its admission predicate is the scoring predicate (Section~\ref{sec:v6replication}); that zero carries no information about the proposer. The quantities that can change are the leakage of the other layers, reviewer misses, what the specification costs, and how much of what it omits a proposer finds. We expect the last to grow with capability, but this paper does not measure it.

\subsection{Prior Work and Architectural Gap}

\para{Authority as state in systems.} Complete mediation, least privilege and tamperproof reference monitors come from capability and operating-system security~\cite{dennis,saltzer,schneider}; authority by conferral rather than identity from object capabilities~\cite{miller2006}; revocation through an indirection the grantor controls from Redell's caretakers~\cite{redell1974}; attenuated delegation from Macaroons~\cite{birgisson2014}. Leases~\cite{gray1989leases} bound authority in time. Zanzibar~\cite{zanzibar} evaluates an authorization check at a snapshot no older than the content it guards, so that a permission already removed is not applied to new content. Lock services hand out sequence numbers that a storage server can check, Chubby's sequencers~\cite{burrows2006chubby} and ZooKeeper's transaction identifiers~\cite{hunt2010zookeeper}, and Kleppmann's fencing token~\cite{kleppmann2016} is the rule that a storage service refuse a writer whose number is stale. The idea is older than any of these: consensus protocols refuse messages from an earlier ballot or term~\cite{lamport1998paxos,ongaro2014raft}, optimistic concurrency control aborts a transaction whose reads have been overwritten~\cite{kung1981occ}, two-phase commit makes a participant's effect wait for a coordinator's decision~\cite{gray1978notes}, HDFS's journal nodes refuse any writer whose epoch is lower than one they have promised~\cite{lipcon2012qjm}, and clustered storage fences a node the cluster has expelled, by removing its key from a SCSI-3 persistent reservation so that the disk refuses its writes~\cite{spc3,fencescsi}, or by powering it off~\cite{robertson2000heartbeat}. Our fence is the stale-epoch rule applied to an agent's authority, with the operator's stop decision in place of an expired lock or a lost election; what is new is not the mechanism but where it is placed and what it is measured against. The gap it closes is the classic time-of-check-to-time-of-use race~\cite{bishop1996toctou}: a check made before the effect, on state that can change before the effect commits. The escrow method~\cite{oneil1986escrow} lets concurrent transactions draw on a shared quantity without serializing on it, which is what a budget shared by concurrent tool calls needs; sagas~\cite{garcia1987sagas} compensate long-lived work that has already committed, which is the only recourse once an effect is out. Machine-checked systems such as seL4~\cite{sel4}, IronFleet~\cite{ironfleet} and Verdi~\cite{verdi} prove properties of executable state machines; we use Verus~\cite{lattuada2023verus,verus}.

\para{Stopping agents.} AI control evaluates untrusted models under trusted monitoring~\cite{control,terekhov,makins,metraudit}, and SHADE-Arena~\cite{shadearena} measures whether agents can complete hidden harmful side tasks without an LLM monitor noticing; resampling protocols~\cite{bhatt2025ctrlz} replace or audit suspicious actions, control games~\cite{griffin2024games} choose which actions to audit, and adaptive macro-protocols~\cite{wen2024adaptive} adjust trust across many tasks where no single action is decisive. These protocols act on proposals and treat a caught action as a stopped one. When a monitor blocks an action before it runs, that action leaves nothing in flight; the window we measure opens when the stop comes later than the dispatch, as when a trajectory is halted after it is flagged, and it holds the agent's other calls already running. Asynchronous monitors, which review actions after they run so as to add no latency, make that the normal case~\cite{stickland2025async}, and the case for monitors that block instead rests on how long an attack takes to do harm after it is dispatched~\cite{lucassen2026blocking}; the fence shortens the harm a late verdict leaves for effects that land at commit. Work on interruptibility asks whether a learning agent will come to resist or avoid being stopped~\cite{orseau2016,omohundro2008,soares2015,offswitch}, and some frontier models given a shutdown mechanism in their own environment have disabled it to finish a task~\cite{schlatter2025shutdown}, which is a reason to put the stop where the agent cannot reach; and work on scheming asks whether a model would hide such behaviour from the evaluations and monitors meant to find it~\cite{safetycase,needham,schoen,korbak}. Our question is the complement of both: once inference has stopped, which of the agent's already dispatched effects still commit, and what refuses them. Evidence that models can fake alignment, keep backdoored behaviour through safety training, or scheme in context~\cite{greenblatt2024faking,hubinger2024sleeper,meinke2024scheming} is the reason not to rest the answer on the proposer's disposition. Proposals for agent infrastructure~\cite{chan2025infra} include mechanisms to attribute, shape and respond to agents' actions, and systems that track and stop model workloads~\cite{controlroom} act where proposals are made; a sink fence is one concrete response mechanism at the other end.

\para{Agent security.} AgentDojo~\cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo,agentdojo} benchmarks prompt injection against tool-using agents. CaMeL~\cite{camel} separates control from data flow and enforces capabilities in an interpreter, FIDES~\cite{costa2025fides} enforces information-flow labels, Progent~\cite{progent} enforces per-call privilege policies, AgentSpec~\cite{agentspec} enforces runtime rules written in a small specification language, Conseca~\cite{conseca} generates a policy for each task's context, IsolateGPT~\cite{isolategpt} isolates the execution of third-party apps, MELON~\cite{melon} flags an action that does not depend on the user's task by re-executing with the task masked, Task Shield~\cite{taskshield} checks each action against the user's goals, ShieldAgent~\cite{chen2025shieldagent} verifies an action trajectory against rules extracted from policy documents, LlamaFirewall~\cite{chennabasappa2025llamafirewall} audits the agent's reasoning for goal hijacking (its AgentDojo scoring counts an attack that succeeded and was flagged afterwards as defended, which assumes the late flag stopped the effect), ToolEmu~\cite{ruan2023toolemu} judges tool-call risk in an emulated sandbox, and a catalogue of design patterns~\cite{beurerkellner2025patterns} constrains what an injected agent can reach by how the application is structured. Each decides whether a call may be made. None commits the effect, so none can refuse, at the moment of commit, an effect admitted under a state that has since changed: a budget another call has spent, a grant its issuer has withdrawn, a stop the operator has ordered. In our scripted races, our own stateless per-call filter, which sees one call and a snapshot of state with no serialization point at commit, overspent a shared budget as soon as two writers contended (Appendix~\ref{sec:systems}); we did not run those systems in the races. These systems and a sink are complementary; a CaMeL-planned call still needs a sink that refuses it after a stop. We did not run CaMeL, whose planner must write programs in its restricted dialect, with the small local models used here. GoEX~\cite{goex} takes the opposite route to a fence, validating actions after they run, with undo and damage confinement. \(\tau\)-bench~\cite{taubench} grades agents on policies that depend on database state, by the database's final state; its policies live in the agent's prompt, not at a sink. Guaranteed Safe AI~\cite{gsai} and shielded or constrained reinforcement learning~\cite{shield,cpo} restrict policies by construction.

\para{Closest prior work, and what is new here.} Three recent papers are close. Zhu and Wang~\cite{ftbudget} conserve budgets across a delegation graph with escrowed reservations bound to lineage, epoch and idempotency key, signed dispatch permits checked at a gateway, and uncertain effects that stay charged until settled; they prove conservation and at-most-once settlement and inject crashes into a two-process SQLite implementation. Our broker and sink use the same mechanisms, which we do not claim as new. Li~\cite{limbo} measures duplicate side effects of agents under injected faults, finds that when a write's outcome cannot be read back the tool contract, not the model, decides whether it is duplicated, and proves that no policy that only verifies can be exactly-once under late commits without a bound on in-flight time. A fence is not such a policy: the surface refuses the late commit itself, so it needs no bound on in-flight time, and it works only where the surface can refuse (Section~\ref{sec:v6boundary}). Moon and Varshney~\cite{moon} verify a containment layer at the typed action boundary, treating the model as an arbitrary adversary. What this paper adds is measurement of three questions those designs raise: how much of an agent's work still commits after each kind of stop, and which term of it each mechanism removes (Section~\ref{sec:v6stop}); whether a reviewer reading proposals can substitute for a check on state (Section~\ref{sec:v6reviewers}); and what a specification written without the benchmark's solutions costs and leaves open, beside a per-task policy of the kind whose utility cost Progent and CaMeL report for their own policies (Section~\ref{sec:v6cost}). We add executable probes of which real surfaces can enforce a fence at all (Section~\ref{sec:v6boundary}).
