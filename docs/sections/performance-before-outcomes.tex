\begin{abstract}
People choose partners before they can observe long-term reliability. We distinguish the information available at a first encounter from what repeated observation can reveal, and examine related predictions in fossil, ethnographic, survey, encounter, and platform records. Across \FosN{} dated hominin crania and the \texttt{LR04} climate record, brain-volume stasis began hundreds of millennia before the late-plateau crises. Renewed growth followed archaeological evidence of fire and cooking, a sequence consistent with a proposed juvenile provisioning mechanism. In partner assessment, the $6'0''$ platform threshold coexists with a near-zero meta-analytic association between male height and completed fertility. Among \PboChkCouplesN{} partnered respondents in \PboChkCountriesN{} countries, partner-rated kindness had a $\PboChkKindFitRatio\times$ larger adjusted association with reported relationship quality than five-trait checklist fit. In \PboJoelDatasetsN{} longitudinal couples datasets, relationship-specific measures explained more baseline variation in satisfaction than static partner traits. Live encounters and surveys also distinguish favorable evaluations of confidence accompanied by warmth from evaluations of unbacked self-promotion. Ethnographic marriage codes describe who chooses and whose approval is required. Testing the proposed assessment mechanism requires measuring what prospective partners and their relatives can learn about conduct, whose judgment shapes the decision, and who can refuse.
\end{abstract}
